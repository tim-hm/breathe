Privacy

Last updated 8 August 2026

The short version

önd has no ads, no analytics, and nothing you are required to sign up for. Your phone is identified to our server by a random ID it generates for itself, and nothing in that ID is derived from you or your device. We store what you tell us during onboarding and what you breathe, so your history and streaks survive a new phone. We sell nothing to anybody.

Two things are worth reading past the summary for. If you subscribe to the coach tier, some of what you told us is sent to Amazon Bedrock so a language model can answer; it is not used to train anything and it is not kept. And önd can record your sessions in Apple Health, and — only if you switch it on — read two heart figures back out to give the coach some context. What the coach receives is in The coach and the language model, and what Health is used for is in Health.

Who we are

önd is made and operated independently by Tim Holmes-Mitra, of Bath, United Kingdom, who is the data controller for the purposes of the UK GDPR. Reach us at support@ondbreathe.app.

Your identity, and the optional sign-in

When you first open önd, the app generates a random identifier and keeps it in your device's Keychain. That identifier is how our server recognises your data on the next request. It is not derived from your hardware, your Apple ID, your phone number, or anything else about you — it is a random number, and it is the entire basis of the claim your device makes.

We never ask for an email address or a password. There is one thing you can sign in to, and it is optional: Sign in with Apple, offered as a row in Settings and never as a gate. Its only purpose is to let you get your history back on a new phone. If you use it, we store one extra thing — the opaque account identifier Apple gives us, which is stable for önd and meaningless to anyone else. We ask Apple for no name and no email address, so Apple sends us neither.

Signing in on a second device folds that device's history into your account and retires the random identifier it started with. Signing out returns the device to a fresh random identifier and leaves the practice already on it alone; what is filed under your Apple account stays there for next time. Deleting your account removes the Apple identifier with everything else.

Until you sign in — which is where the app starts, and where most people will stay — the practical consequence is worth stating plainly: because we hold no way to identify you as a person, we cannot recover your history if you lose the device and the identifier with it, and we cannot confirm over email that a record is yours without that identifier.

What we store

Everything below is held against that random identifier and nothing else.

What you tell us during onboarding
Your goals, how much experience you have with breathwork, and how often you want reminders. Optionally — each one can be left blank, and blank is the default — a decade-wide birth-year band, your gender, a display name, and a short free-text note about what brought you here.
What you breathe
For each completed session: which technique, when it started, how long it ran, how many cycles and breaths, and whether it finished or you ended it early. Nothing treats an early end as a failure. If you use the breath-hold measurement, we store the number of seconds and when you measured it.
Your subscription, if you have one
The App Store transaction identifier for your subscription and the date it runs until, so the server knows what you have paid for. Payment itself happens entirely through Apple — we never see your card, your billing address, or your Apple ID.
Exercises you write yourself
If you compose your own breathing exercise, we store the name you gave it, the goal you chose, and its stages and timings, so it survives a new phone too. It is yours alone: nobody else can read it, it never appears on a leaderboard, and the coach is told only that you practised "another exercise" — never what you called it.
A daily counter, if you use the coach
One number per day: how many language-model calls you have made. It exists to cap what the model costs us.
What our server logs
One line for each request your app makes: which operation it was, whether it succeeded, how long it took, and your random identifier. Coach calls add the size of the request and the reply, counted in tokens — never any of the words. These are operational logs, kept to tell us why something broke. They are capped by size and pushed out by newer ones rather than deleted on a schedule, and deleting your account does not reach them.

Reminders are off until you set one up. The app asks iOS for notification permission at the moment you add your first schedule, and never before. The schedules themselves stay on your device — we never see them.

The coach and the language model

The coach tier answers using a large language model. We do not run that model ourselves — calls go from our server straight to Amazon Bedrock, Amazon Web Services' own model service, with no broker or reseller in between. The credentials for those calls stay on our server and never ship inside the app.

To answer usefully, the request sent to Bedrock includes some of what you have told us:

  • Your goals and your stated experience level
  • Your birth-year band and gender, if you gave them, because published breath-hold reference ranges differ by both
  • Your free-text note, in your own words, if you wrote one
  • Recent practice context and your breath-hold scores, if you have recorded any
  • A coarse heart summary, if you turned that setting on — see Health below
  • The question or technique you asked about

Your display name is never sent. Neither is your identifier, so there is nothing at the other end to attach one conversation to another with. Treat the free-text note as the field to be thoughtful about — it is the one place you can type anything at all, and it is included verbatim.

We store none of it. Prompts, replies, and explanations are never written to our database and never written to a log — the daily counter described above, and a log line recording that a call happened and how large it was, are the whole of what survives a coach conversation on our side. Nor is it stored at the other end. Amazon handles coach requests on our instructions and under our contract with them: your words are not used to train or improve any model, they are not retained once the answer has come back, and the company that built the model never receives them.

The coach gives general wellness guidance about breathing. It is not a doctor, it does not diagnose, and nothing it says is medical advice.

Health

önd both writes to Apple Health and, if you ask it to, reads two figures back out. Neither is ever stored on our server, and the reading half is off until you switch it on.

What we write to Health
Each session you keep is recorded on your device as Mindful Minutes — when it started and how long it ran, and nothing else. Your iPhone and your Apple Watch each write the sessions they ran. iOS asks your permission the first time there are minutes to credit; declining costs you nothing but the entry.
What we read, only if you turn it on
Settings → Let the coach see heart trends is off until you switch it on, and switching it on is what asks Health for read access — to two figures, your resting heart rate and your heart-rate variability, and to nothing else. Nothing is read before you do, and turning it back off means the very next coach request carries nothing.
What actually leaves your phone
Not your readings. Your phone reduces the last eight weeks to whole numbers — a seven-day average, and where there is enough history a single number for how it has moved — and only those travel, attached to the coach request you just made. No dates, no individual readings, and nothing at all when the setting is off.

We store none of it, anywhere. The heart summary exists on our server for the length of one coach request, goes into that request to Bedrock, and is dropped with it: it is never written to our database and never written to a log. Health data is the one category the server is deliberately built to be incapable of keeping.

Because this is health data, we rely on your explicit consent for it, which is exactly what that switch is — and you can withdraw it by switching it off. That does not revoke Health's own permission, which is Apple's to hold: revoke it in the Health app if you want to.

Leaderboards are opt-in

You appear on a leaderboard only if you choose a display name. Until you set one there is no name to show, and the query that builds every board skips your row entirely — that is a structural rule in the code, not a preference we honour.

If you do set one, other people see that name and the figure the board ranks — minutes breathed, streak length, or best breath-hold. They never see your other answers, your note, or your session history. Clearing your display name takes you back off the boards.

What we never do

  • No advertising, and no ad identifiers
  • No third-party analytics, attribution, or crash-reporting SDK — the only outside code in the app is the two open-source libraries it uses to talk to our own server, one for the network calls and one for the message format
  • No selling, renting, or sharing your data with anyone for their own purposes
  • No tracking you across other apps or websites
  • No location, contacts, photos, or microphone — the app never asks for any of them
  • No reading anything from Health beyond the two heart figures described in Health, and none of those unless you switch them on

Where your data lives

On one server in Amazon Web Services' London region (eu-west-2), in the United Kingdom. A nightly backup of the database is written to Amazon's storage service in that same London region, encrypted, and deleted automatically after 30 days.

Coach requests are the one thing that can travel. We send them to Bedrock's London endpoint, and Bedrock may forward any individual request to another of its European regions when it needs the capacity — Ireland, Frankfurt, Paris, Stockholm, Milan, or Spain. So a coach request may leave the United Kingdom; it never leaves the United Kingdom and the European Economic Area, it stays inside Amazon Web Services throughout, and nothing about it is stored at either end.

How long we keep it

Your profile, your practice history and the exercises you wrote are kept until you erase them — a history you can still see is the point of storing it at all. Sessions you delete in the app are deleted from the server on the next sync. Coach conversations are never stored, so there is nothing to retain.

The two exceptions to "until you erase it" are the ones described above: the nightly database backup, which holds whatever was there at the time and is deleted after 30 days, and the server logs, which hold no more than the line described above and are pushed out by newer requests rather than kept to a date.

Your rights, and how to erase everything

Under the UK GDPR you can ask us for a copy of what we hold about you, ask us to correct it, ask us to erase it, or object to our processing it. Most of the data we hold you can also edit directly in the app.

You can erase everything yourself, without asking us and without waiting for a reply: Settings → Delete account → Delete everything. On our side it removes your profile, your sessions, your breath-hold scores, the exercises you wrote, your coach counter, and the Apple sign-in link if you made one — and then the row itself. On yours it clears the copies held on your iPhone and on a paired Apple Watch, including your reminder schedules and any notifications they had queued with iOS.

It is a deletion, not a flag: nothing in the app or on the server can bring it back. The one qualification is the nightly backup described above, which still holds whatever was there when it ran until it ages out within 30 days, and which we open only to restore the whole database after a failure.

Deleting your account does not cancel an önd subscription. Only Apple can do that — from Manage subscription in the app, or under your name in the system Settings.

If you would rather we did it, email support@ondbreathe.app. Because we hold no name or email address for you, we need the random identifier your device holds in order to find your record, and we will tell you how to retrieve it when you write.

Deleting the app alone does not erase what is on the server, and because the identifier lives in the Keychain it can survive a reinstall — so if erasure is what you want, use the control in the app or write to us.

If you think we have handled your data badly, please tell us first so we can put it right. You also have the right to complain to the Information Commissioner's Office at ico.org.uk.

Our lawful bases

We process your onboarding answers, your practice history, and the exercises you write to provide the app you asked us to provide, which is performance of a contract. We process your subscription details for the same reason, and the Apple account identifier, if you sign in, to give you back the history you asked us to hold for you.

Your optional answers — birth-year band, gender, your note, your display name — are consent, given by choosing to fill them in and withdrawn by clearing them. The heart trends the coach can see are health data, which the UK GDPR treats as a special category and which therefore rests on your explicit consent: the switch in Settings is that consent, turning it off withdraws it, and we process those figures for nothing else.

The daily coach counter rests on our legitimate interest in not being bankrupted by a runaway bill. Our server logs and the nightly backup rest on our legitimate interest in being able to tell why something broke, and in not losing everybody's history to one failed disk.

Children

önd is not directed at children under 13, and we do not knowingly collect data from them. If you believe a child has given us data, email support@ondbreathe.app and we will erase it.
